First: what does “work on a project” mean?

It means you and the interviewer will solve a small work problem together. “Project” describes the exercise; it does not automatically mean software development.

Possible marketing outputs

A campaign idea, a short launch plan, landing-page copy, social content, a customer journey, a measurement recommendation, an event follow-up sequence, or a small prototype.

When you should code

Only when the brief asks for a working page, prototype, data task, or another technical deliverable. Ask whether they want a concept, a draft, or a working implementation before you start building.

What they can observe

How you clarify the goal, choose an audience, use evidence, make trade-offs, communicate while working, respond to feedback, and check whether the result is useful.

What to build beforehand

Practice exercises and a reliable working setup. Don’t prebuild a guessed answer. You do not yet know their actual brief, constraints, or requested output.

Use this order: understand the problem → agree on the output → choose a direction → make the deliverable → review it. If the assignment is ambiguous, asking one useful question is better than confidently solving the wrong problem.

A steady method for the hour

Use this as a flexible rhythm, not a rigid timer. If the interviewer changes direction or gives feedback, pause and adapt. Keep your reasoning audible while you work.

00–05

Read, restate, and clarify

Summarize the goal in your own words. Ask who the audience is, what action matters, which channel or constraints apply, and what format they expect.

05–12

Separate facts from assumptions

Write down what the brief gives you, what you still need, and the assumptions you can safely make. Label assumptions instead of presenting them as research.

12–18

Choose one direction

Offer a couple of plausible routes, compare them against the goal, and recommend one. Explain the trade-off in one or two sentences.

18–40

Make the requested thing

Produce the smallest complete deliverable: copy, plan, journey, analysis, prototype, or code as specified. Use AI for a bounded task and keep ownership of the decisions.

40–50

Review claims, clarity, and usability

Check every factual product statement, audience fit, call to action, constraints, and obvious edge cases. Remove unsupported claims and filler.

50–56

Say how you would measure and improve it

Pick one primary success metric, one useful guardrail, and a sensible next experiment or learning step. Don’t invent performance results.

56–60

Walk them through the result

State the goal, recommendation, deliverable, evidence or assumptions, and next step. Invite one final question or piece of feedback.

Useful opening line: “Let me make sure I understand the outcome you want. Who is the priority audience, what should they do next, and what would make this exercise successful?”

Keep the work organized with one canvas

Use these prompts in a blank document or notebook. You do not need a fancy template or a slide deck for a one-hour exercise.

Outcome

What business or user outcome does the brief ask for?

Audience + problem

Who is this for, what do they care about, and what is blocking them?

Known / assumed

Which points come from the brief or a source? Which are hypotheses?

Recommendation

What is the one direction you recommend, and why this one?

Deliverable

What exact output did the interviewer request? Is it finished enough to review?

Measure + next

What would success look like, what could go wrong, and what would you learn next?

A strong marketing chain is: audience need → relevant value → credible proof → clear next action → measurable learning.

Pick tools for the task in front of you

Choose a small working set. Tools help you get evidence or produce the requested format; they do not replace judgment.

For a campaign or copy task

  • Brief + notes in a doc
  • AI agent for options and editing
  • Marketing Skills: product-marketing, copywriting
  • Official Ostorlab pages for product facts

Keep a source beside every factual claim. A spreadsheet is useful only if you need to compare options or track a plan.

For analytics practice

Google Analytics’ demo account lets you explore sample GA4 data without changing your own account. Search Console reports can show clicks, impressions, CTR, and average position—but only for a property you can access.

For trend or visual work

Google Trends is directional, normalized interest—not absolute search volume. Canva is useful if a visual is explicitly requested. Don’t spend interview time designing slides when a clear written outline is enough.

Use AI as a teammate you direct

The invitation allows AI coding tools. A marketing assignment may need a general AI assistant or agent rather than a coding agent. Use one you already know; say what it did and verify the output.

Good uses

Summarize a long brief, surface questions, generate alternative angles, edit for clarity, check calculations, flag unsupported claims, review edge cases, or create a small first draft.

Your job stays yours

Choose the audience, goal, recommendation, evidence standard, trade-offs, and final wording. Correct the agent if it misunderstands the brief. Don’t present invented research as real customer evidence.

Think: one driver, a few focused jobs. You do not need a swarm of agents. Have one agent analyze the brief, another pass review the draft if time allows, and make the final decision yourself. Avoid feeding private or confidential material into an external tool.

Prompt 1 · Turn the brief into a work plan

Use at the start. Paste only the interview brief or permitted information.

Act as a careful marketing project partner. First, restate the requested outcome and deliverable. Then list: (1) facts explicitly provided, (2) assumptions, (3) the 3 most important clarifying questions, (4) risks or constraints, and (5) a small step-by-step plan that fits the time available. Do not invent customer research, product capabilities, or performance data. If something is unknown, label it unknown.

Prompt 2 · Generate and compare message options

Use after agreeing on the goal and audience.

Given this audience, goal, approved product facts, and channel: suggest 3 genuinely different message angles. For each, explain the audience insight, core promise, evidence needed, likely objection, and best next action. Mark any unsupported claim as [NEEDS PROOF]. Recommend one option against the stated goal, but leave the final choice to me. Keep the language concrete and avoid security guarantees unless the brief supplies proof.

Prompt 3 · Review copy for trust and clarity

Use on a draft, then personally accept or reject each suggestion.

Review this draft for the specified audience and action. Flag vague language, jargon, unclear benefits, missing proof, unsupported factual claims, confusing CTA, and mismatch with the brief. Do not rewrite everything. Give the 5 highest-impact fixes first, explain why each matters, and show a revised version that preserves only supported facts. Tell me what you could not verify.

Prompt 4 · Check a metric or campaign table

Use for arithmetic and interpretation; verify the math yourself.

Check these campaign figures. Show the formula and denominator for every calculated rate or cost. Identify inconsistent totals, missing data, and comparisons that would be misleading. Separate observed facts from possible explanations. Recommend one next analysis or bounded experiment. Do not claim causation from a simple before/after comparison.

Prompt 5 · Build only if the brief asks for a prototype

Use this only if they explicitly request a working page, prototype, or code deliverable.

Before writing code, restate the requested user, goal, required behavior, and constraints. Ask only critical questions. Build the smallest working prototype that meets the brief; do not invent APIs, claims, analytics, or integrations. Keep copy editable, use accessible labels and keyboard focus, and tell me how to run and inspect the result. After building, list what works, what is incomplete, and the next highest-value improvement.

Don’t let AI use consume the hour. If the setup fails, continue with your own notes. If you use AI, state the task you gave it, check the response, and explain what you changed.

Mock practice: a small B2B security campaign

This is a practice scenario only—not Ostorlab’s disclosed interview assignment. Use official company information as the source of facts and label any audience insight as a hypothesis unless you have evidence.

Practice brief: “Help a security team understand why they should consider a security assessment for their mobile app. Propose one useful campaign asset and explain how you’d judge whether it worked.” Give yourself 35 minutes to create, then 5 minutes to present.

Step 1 · Narrow it

Ask which security role, which action (for example, learn more or request a demo), which channel, and what proof is approved. If unanswered, state your assumptions.

Step 2 · Make it credible

Use only product facts supported by current official Ostorlab materials. Do not promise that a product “prevents breaches” or guarantees security unless the brief explicitly provides substantiation.

Step 3 · Produce one asset

Choose a single format, such as a landing-page outline or short campaign email. Make the audience, problem, value, proof, and next action easy to spot.

Step 4 · Measure and explain

Name one primary metric connected to the action, one quality guardrail, and one next test. Explain what you would need to learn before spending money or scaling.

Finish with a 45-second walkthrough: “I focused on [audience] because [reason/assumption]. I recommend [idea] to help them [action]. The key proof is [approved evidence]. I’d measure [metric] and check [guardrail]. The biggest unknown is [unknown], so my next step would be [test or research].”

Be ready to talk through your decisions

The work matters, and so does how you think with another person. Practise answers that are short, specific, and honest.

“Tell me about your approach.”

Explain how you clarified the objective, identified the audience, chose your direction, and checked the result. Give a real example from your submitted project if it fits.

“Why did you choose this idea?”

Connect the choice to the audience, goal, constraints, and evidence. Mention one trade-off and what information could change your mind.

“How did you use AI?”

Say what task you delegated, how you checked the output, and what judgment or editing you contributed. Don’t claim the tool performed work it did not do.

“What would you do with more time?”

Choose the next step that reduces the biggest uncertainty: customer evidence, claim validation, a usability check, or a properly scoped experiment.

Useful questions to ask the interviewer

  • About the task: “What is the most important outcome you want from this exercise?”
  • About the audience: “Is there a particular buyer or user segment I should prioritize?”
  • About the output: “Would you prefer a rough recommendation, a polished asset, or a working prototype?”
  • About feedback: “Would you like me to talk through decisions as I work?”
  • At the end: “What would you expect a successful version of this work to achieve?”

Get ready without cramming

Use these as independent sessions whenever you have time. The goal is several focused repetitions, not building a finished campaign in advance.

Practice session A · Understand the examples

Read the repository overview and one marketing brief. Summarize the goal, audience, deliverable, evidence, and risks in your own words.

Practice session B · Make a first deliverable

Timebox one brief. Use a blank document. Clarify, choose, produce, review, and present. Keep the AI closed for the first pass if you tend to over-rely on it.

Practice session C · Add tools intentionally

Repeat a different brief with one AI agent. Try one marketing skill only if already installed. Compare the result with your own thinking and note corrections.

Practice session D · Explain under time pressure

Give a 2-minute walkthrough and answer “why this?”, “what is uncertain?”, and “how would you measure it?” out loud. Ask someone to give you one piece of feedback.
